Deploying a Brochure: Designing with English to Explain What I Study

Evidence GA1-240202501-AA1-EV03 — A visual brochure presenting the Software Analysis and Development program at SENA. Short text, clear structure, and the challenge of saying exactly the right thing in very few words.

2026-10-11 • 4 min read • GA1-240202501-AA1-EV03

In software, a README is the first thing anyone reads about your project. It has to communicate purpose, context, and value in the shortest possible space — because nobody reads a long README. A brochure is the same contract applied to design: maximum clarity, minimum words, zero ambiguity. Writing one in English adds an extra constraint on top of the design challenge.

For this evidence I produced a visual brochure in English presenting the Software Analysis and Development program at SENA (GA1-240202501-AA1-EV03). The document had to introduce who I am, explain what the program covers, state my goals, and cite its source — all in a single-page layout that a reader can absorb in under a minute.

Why brochure writing is a different kind of commit

Writing a brochure in English is not about fluency — it is about compression. Every sentence has to earn its space. In the brochure above, the introduction is two sentences. The program description is two sentences. The goals section is two sentences. Six sentences to describe a two-year technical program, a personal motivation, and a professional aspiration. That is a tighter constraint than most code reviews.

In developer terms, this is the challenge of writing a good commit message: you have to convey what changed, why it matters, and what it connects to — in one line if possible, two if necessary. Brochure writing trains the same muscle. It forces you to identify what is essential and discard everything that is just noise.

The design constraint: English in a visual format

A brochure adds a constraint that a plain essay does not have: the text must work with the layout, not against it. A sentence that is grammatically correct but too long will break a column. A heading that is accurate but vague will fail to anchor the reader's eye. This forces a discipline that pure writing practice does not — you have to think about how the words will land visually, not just whether they are correct linguistically.

That is a skill that transfers directly to professional work. Documentation, READMEs, slide decks, dashboards — every technical output that reaches another human has a layout, and the words have to fit it. This evidence is the first in this blog to make that constraint explicit.
